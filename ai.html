AI and Its Economic Impact

A growing part of my research studies how artificial intelligence is reshaping the economy — and how we should measure it. I use task-based exposure measures, large administrative datasets, structural models, and frontier language models to ask where AI's effects show up first: in credit, in wages, in household behaviour, and in the choices people make about their careers. A recurring theme is the gap between exposure and incidence — what a technology could affect versus where its effects actually land — and the care needed when using AI itself as a measurement instrument.

Below are working notes from an ongoing empirical project, followed by projects in progress.

Notes

September 2026 · Working notes

Where is AI showing up in the data?

Since ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, the question I hear most is whether generative AI is already changing the economy. I have looked for it in a dozen places: mortgage and business credit, jobs and wages, job vacancies in three countries, how people spend their time, mental-health care, marriage and births, admission to university programmes, and industry prices. The short version: one signal keeps showing up, in mortgage refinancing. Almost everywhere else, the honest answer is not yet.

In this note: Measuring exposure · The mortgage signal · Where else I looked · Why “not yet” matters · What’s next

Measuring exposure

Everything below starts from an AI-exposure score for each occupation: roughly, how much the abilities a job requires overlap with what current AI, and language models in particular, can do. I aggregate these scores to industries and to US counties in two ways — by where people work (the county's 2019 industry mix) and by where people live (the 2019 occupations of employed residents). The two are less alike than one might expect: across counties they correlate at only about 0.26.

Exposure is not adoption, and it is not job loss; it measures where AI could matter. As a check, I compare it with what people actually do with AI, using Google's occupation-level Gemini usage data and Anthropic's Claude usage data. Those usage data were collected in 2026, after most of the outcomes below, so they help validate the measure rather than prove any effect.

The one signal that keeps showing up: mortgage refinancing

The US mortgage data (HMDA) record home-loan applications lender by lender and county by county. Comparing 2019 with 2023–2025 across about 1.6 million lender-county-year cells, more AI-exposed places see less mortgage lending. That broad pattern is not, by itself, an AI effect: high-exposure counties are large, tech-heavy, remote-work-intensive places whose mortgage markets move with the rate cycle. Once I control for rate sensitivity, local geography and proxies for household balance sheets, the broad gap largely disappears.

What survives is a sharper comparison. Within the same county and year, and for the same lender, refinance lending falls more than purchase lending in more AI-exposed counties: about 14% fewer refinance loans, relative to purchase loans, per standard deviation of exposure (a Poisson estimate; the 95% interval is roughly 10–18%). Three things make me take this seriously:

  • It survives proxies for the obvious confounder. Controlling for interest-rate lock-in (each county's 2020–21 refinancing patterns, plus machine-learning predictions of its rate sensitivity) shrinks the refinance gap by about a third on the transformed-count scale, but it stays clearly negative.
  • It holds up on revised data. Re-estimating the core specification on the revised 2024 HMDA file and the 2025 snapshot leaves the estimate essentially unchanged.
  • Where borrowers live matters. In separate regressions where both measures enter, the fall in mortgage lending loads mainly on residents' exposure — the people whose income repays the loan — rather than on local employers'. A resident measure built from Google's 2026 usage data also predicts lower lending, though only as a check after the fact.

There are also clear limits to what these data can say. Purchase lending barely moves, so this is not a credit crunch. HMDA cannot tell lenders screening more carefully apart from borrowers choosing not to refinance. A narrower story I initially expected — lenders pulling back from large loans just above the conforming limit (the ceiling for loans Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can buy) — did not hold up. And early changes in credit do not reliably predict later unemployment once differences across states are accounted for. The finding is a persistent reallocation of refinancing, not yet evidence that lenders are pricing AI risk.

Why “not yet” is still informative

AI can change expected risk much faster than it changes jobs. Tasks can be reorganised in months, while employment, firm counts and household income move slowly — and credit markets can, in principle, respond to expectations before any of those move. The evidence so far fits that timing, though it cannot show what lenders expected: a signal in refinancing, and little yet in employment, firms or prices. Some of the nulls are also tight enough to be informative in their own right. For county employment growth between early 2024 and early 2026, the estimates rule out declines larger than about half a percent per standard deviation of exposure.

A second lesson concerns the exposure scores themselves. A single score treats every exposed task alike, but in a task-based model where AI replaces some work and creates other work inside firms, what matters for wages is which tasks are exposed. Calibrating that model to US industries with O*NET task content and OEWS employment, a single score and the full task profile disagree on the sign of the predicted wage effect in about one industry in nine today, and in roughly 30% of cases in stylised scenarios where AI capabilities become more task-specific. For now, the extra task detail improves the fit to observed industry wages only modestly, and not significantly. If capabilities do diverge, though, a single exposure number would become a less reliable guide to who is affected.

What I am watching next

  • Loan performance. Whether the refinancing shift anticipates later delinquencies — the test that would separate careful screening from realised risk.
  • University placements. Turkey's 2026 placement results, to see whether early shifts in the selectivity of exposed programmes settle into a pattern.
  • Usage data. New releases from AI developers that measure adoption directly, rather than exposure.

Projects

Monetary Policy Communication and LLM-Simulated Household Spending
Work in progress · preliminary draft available on request
How does the way a central bank explains a policy move change how households respond to it? I build a "persona economy" of several hundred synthetic households with realistic balance sheets and use frontier language models to elicit how each would adjust spending, saving, and borrowing under different policy communications — holding the policy action fixed and varying only the narrative around it. The project treats the language model as a social-science instrument and is explicit about what simulated responses can and cannot tell us about real behaviour.
The Economics of AI Competition: Vertical Differentiation, Ecosystems, and Strategic Open Source
Work in progress · preliminary draft available on request
Why does frontier AI stay so concentrated even though knowledge diffuses quickly and switching costs are low? This project identifies two reinforcing forces. Firms with complementary ecosystems face a larger prize in the quality race, because ecosystem profits flow disproportionately to the leader — an advantage I microfound in the neural scaling laws that link compute to model quality. And a data flywheel lets today's winner start tomorrow's race ahead. The model then rationalises strategic behaviour we observe in the industry — why an ecosystem firm might release a free model to compress rivals' niches, and why it would resist distillation of its open weights — and its predictions map to quantities measurable from training runs, firm financials, and post-training ablations.
